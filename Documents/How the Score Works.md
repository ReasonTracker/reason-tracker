# How the Score Works

Most debates are hard to follow because reasons get shouted, not weighed. You hear ten arguments, but not which ones actually hold up, which ones matter most, or where the disagreement really is.

Reason Tracker draws a decision as a map, and scores it the same way every time.

## 1. Everything starts out standing

By default, every claim starts at full standing — not because it's proven, but because nothing in the map has knocked it down yet. Standing is not truth. It's just "not yet successfully challenged."

Where a claim has no reasons attached yet and full standing would be misleading, its starting score can be set openly on the map instead — a visible placeholder, not a hidden adjustment. As soon as real reasons are connected to it, the placeholder is discarded and the reasons take over.

## 2. Reasons are claims too

A reason for or against the decision is itself a claim — so it can be supported, and it can be attacked. Nothing gets to be a knockout argument just by sounding confident. It has to survive its own challenges.

## 3. A reason only hits as hard as it stands

This is the heart of it. If a strong-sounding objection collapses under scrutiny, it barely moves the score. If an attack is badly weakened, the claim it was attacking recovers. Defences don't score points directly — they work by weakening the attacks they answer.

## 4. True is not the same as important

A reason can be completely correct and still barely matter to this decision. So the map tracks relevance separately: reasons that matter more carry more weight into the decision, reasons that matter less carry less — and that judgement is on the map too, where it can be challenged like anything else.

## 5. The score flows inward

Scores are worked out from the outer reasons in toward the decision. Each claim's standing comes from the reasons pointing at it — supports pulling it up, attacks pulling it down, each weighted by how well it stands and how relevant it is. The decision's scoreboard is just the end of that flow: how the reasons, as they currently stand, balance for and against.

## 6. A decision can flip

Some claims are reversible: an either/or choice stated in one direction. If the reasons drive a reversible claim far enough below zero, it doesn't just lose — it flips, and the map now stands behind the opposite statement, with the strength the reasons actually earned. A decision that started as "choose permanent Daylight Saving Time" can become "choose permanent Standard Time" — not by anyone editing the conclusion, but by the reasons underneath it changing.

## 7. If it isn't in the map, it doesn't count

No hidden penalties, no credit for evidence nobody has entered, no points for volume. Three weak reasons don't beat one strong one. And if you think something is missing, that's not a flaw in the score — that's your contribution: add it, connect it, and watch what actually changes.

## The score is a snapshot, not a verdict

It is not a poll, not a probability that something is true, and not an AI deciding for you. It's a picture of the argument as entered so far, scored by rules you can inspect. Disagree with the result? The map shows you exactly where to push: challenge a claim, challenge its support, or challenge its relevance — and the score updates, in the open, for everyone to see.

That's the point of Reason Tracker: not to end arguments, but to make them locatable.
